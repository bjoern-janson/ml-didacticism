# Genesis 3 — Legibility-First Structural Decoding

**Source:** pinned King James Version (KJV) corpus  
**Purpose:** make the chapter legible before interpretation  
**Status:** working structural reading; categories may evolve when later text requires them

The governing boundary is:

```math
\boxed{\text{TEXT} \neq \text{STRUCTURAL PARSE} \neq \text{INTERPRETATION}}
```

The working objective is simple:

```math
\boxed{
\text{make the wording legible}
\rightarrow
\text{preserve recoverable structure}
\rightarrow
\text{notice patterns}
\rightarrow
\text{interpret only afterward}
}
```

Genesis 3 is structurally different from Genesis 1–2. The chapter is organized around **competing statements about a rule and its consequence, human evaluation and action, resulting state changes, concealment, inquiry, attribution, imposed consequences, and changed access to the environment**.

A first-pass chapter compression is:

```math
\boxed{
\text{rule / represented consequence}
\rightarrow
\text{competing claims}
\rightarrow
\text{evaluation}
\rightarrow
\text{action}
\rightarrow
\text{state change}
\rightarrow
\text{concealment}
\rightarrow
\text{inquiry / attribution}
\rightarrow
\text{consequences}
\rightarrow
\text{access restriction}
}
```

This is a structural summary, not a claim about the chapter's ultimate theological meaning.

---

# 1. Genesis 3:1–5 — Competing representations of the rule and its consequence

## Plain rendering

The serpent questions the woman about what God said regarding eating from the garden. The woman answers that fruit from the garden may be eaten, except for the fruit of a particular tree; she attributes a prohibition and a possible death consequence to God. The serpent directly contradicts the death statement and gives an alternative account of what eating will lead to: opened eyes and knowledge of good and evil.

## Actors

```text
serpent
woman
God / LORD God as the source discussed inside the conversation
```

## Structural objects

The conversation contains several distinct represented objects:

```text
permission:
  eating fruit from garden trees is represented as permitted

prohibition:
  eating fruit from the specified tree is represented as prohibited

additional reported prohibition:
  touching the fruit/tree is represented by the woman as prohibited

represented consequence A:
  prohibited interaction → death

competing represented consequence B:
  eating → eyes opened
         → being “as gods”
         → knowing good and evil
```

The important structural fact is that the chapter now contains **multiple representations of the same prospective action and its consequences**.

```math
\boxed{
\text{same candidate action}
\rightarrow
\{\text{competing represented outcomes}\}
}
```

The text does not require us to assign a formal psychological belief state to every speaker. What it directly gives is speech containing incompatible claims.

## Information-source structure

The woman reports what God said. The serpent asks about and then contradicts the represented prior statement.

So the chapter contains explicit source structure:

```text
God → attributed prior command/consequence
woman → report of that command/consequence
serpent → challenge / competing statement
```

That is already more complex than a flat list of propositions because **who says what** matters.

## OPEN / do not force

- the serpent's private intent;
- whether the woman internally believes every element of her report;
- why her wording differs from the earlier wording in Genesis 2;
- a mechanism connecting eating to either stated future outcome;
- the meaning of “as gods” beyond the words supplied.

# 2. Genesis 3:6 — Evaluation becomes action

## Plain rendering

The woman looks at the tree and evaluates it positively in several ways: good for food, pleasant to the eyes, and desirable for gaining wisdom. She takes its fruit, eats, gives some to her husband, and he eats.

## Properties / evaluations represented in the text

```text
tree → good for food
tree → pleasant to the eyes
tree → desired to make one wise
```

These are not merely environmental facts in the abstract. The verse presents them in relation to the woman's seeing/evaluation of the tree.

## Actions

```text
woman → takes fruit
woman → eats fruit
woman → gives fruit → husband
husband → eats fruit
```

## Sequence

```math
\boxed{
\text{perception / evaluation}
\rightarrow
\text{taking}
\rightarrow
\text{eating}
\rightarrow
\text{giving}
\rightarrow
\text{eating by husband}
}
```

The temporal/subordinate wording matters: the evaluation is represented before the action sequence.

But sequence alone does not identify a complete internal decision mechanism.

## New structural category: evaluation-to-action boundary

Genesis 1 repeatedly supplied explicit evaluations of resulting states. Genesis 3 now gives an agent-level evaluation immediately adjacent to a performed action.

That creates a useful distinction:

```math
\boxed{
\text{represented evaluation}
\neq
\text{performed action}
}
```

while still preserving their textual ordering.

## OPEN

- which evaluation, if any, controlled the decision;
- whether all three evaluations had equal importance;
- the complete set of alternatives the woman considered;
- the husband's reasoning or evaluation before eating.

# 3. Genesis 3:7–10 — State change, self-representation, covering, hiding

## Plain rendering

After both eat, their eyes are described as opened and they know that they are naked. They sew fig leaves together and make coverings for themselves. Later they hear the LORD God in the garden and hide among the trees. Adam says that he heard God's voice, was afraid because he was naked, and hid.

## Reported state changes

```text
eyes of both → opened
both → know they are naked
```

The first of these directly corresponds in wording to part of the serpent's earlier prospective statement:

```text
3:5  “your eyes shall be opened”
3:7  “the eyes of them both were opened”
```

That is a **textual correspondence**. Recording it does not by itself settle the truth of every surrounding claim or identify a mechanism.

## Actions following the state change

```text
both → sew fig leaves
both → make aprons / coverings
both → hear the LORD God in the garden
both → hide among trees
```

The chapter therefore moves from action to changed self-representation and then to new behavior:

```math
\boxed{
\text{eat}
\rightarrow
\text{reported changed state / knowledge}
\rightarrow
\text{covering behavior}
\rightarrow
\text{hiding behavior}
}
```

Narrative order is explicit. Full causal mechanism is not.

## Explicit reason relation in Adam's account

Adam says:

```text
I was afraid because I was naked
```

So the text gives a direct reported reason edge:

```math
\boxed{
\text{reported nakedness}
\xrightarrow{\text{because}}
\text{reported fear}
}
```

He also reports hiding, but the sentence does not explicitly say `because I was afraid, I hid`. That stronger edge can remain unasserted.

## Environmental predicates worth retaining

The text also gives:

```text
LORD God → walking in garden
setting → cool of the day
Adam + wife → hidden among garden trees
```

These are ordinary descriptive relations and do not need a special theory to be preserved.

# 4. Genesis 3:11–13 — Inquiry, source tracing, action tracing, attribution

## Plain rendering

The LORD God asks Adam who told him that he was naked and whether he ate from the prohibited tree. Adam says that the woman God gave him gave him fruit and that he ate. The woman is then asked what she did; she says that the serpent beguiled her and that she ate.

## Query structure

The chapter now explicitly asks two different diagnostic questions:

```text
information-source query:
  Who told you that you were naked?

action query:
  Did you eat from the tree that was prohibited?
```

These should not be collapsed.

One asks about the **source of represented information**.
The other asks about a **performed action**.

## Adam's account

```text
God → gave woman → to/with Adam
woman → gave fruit → Adam
Adam → ate
```

The account therefore traces an upstream chain while still ending in Adam's explicit admission of action.

The text does not itself require labels such as:

```text
blame
excuse
exoneration
responsibility transfer
```

Those are possible interpretations of the discourse, not necessary structural primitives at this layer.

## Woman's account

```text
serpent → beguiled woman
woman → ate
```

`beguiled` is stronger than mere chronological precedence: it is an influence/deception attribution made in the woman's report.

But:

```math
\boxed{
\text{reported influence attribution}
\neq
\text{fully specified mechanism}
}
```

## New chapter-level pattern: backward tracing

Genesis 3 repeatedly moves backward from an observed state toward prior information and action:

```math
\boxed{
\text{current state}
\rightarrow
\text{source query}
\rightarrow
\text{prior action query}
\rightarrow
\text{upstream account}
}
```

That is a distinct structural operation from the forward transformations dominating Genesis 1.

# 5. Genesis 3:14–19 — Action-linked consequences and changed conditions

## Plain rendering

The LORD God addresses the serpent, the woman, and Adam in turn. The speech explicitly links prior actions to changed conditions and future relations. The serpent is cursed and assigned future conditions; enmity is introduced between serpent/woman and their seed. The woman is told of multiplied sorrow in conception/childbirth and a changed husband-wife relation. Adam is told that because he listened to his wife and ate from the prohibited tree, the ground is cursed; food production becomes associated with sorrow, thorns, thistles, labor, and eventual return to the ground/dust.

## Explicit reason / consequence structure

The chapter contains direct `because` relations:

```text
serpent:
  “Because thou hast done this ...”

Adam:
  because you listened to your wife
  and ate from the prohibited tree
  → cursed ground / changed future conditions
```

So here the text itself licenses a stronger edge than mere sequence:

```math
\boxed{
\text{named prior action}
\xrightarrow{\text{explicit because}}
\text{stated changed condition / consequence}
}
```

## Consequence classes appearing in the passage

```text
status change:
  serpent → cursed

environment / bodily condition:
  belly-going, dust-eating

relation change:
  enmity introduced

future human condition:
  sorrow / conception / childbirth
  desire / rule relation

agent-environment relation:
  ground cursed in relation to Adam
  food production associated with sorrow and labor
  thorns / thistles

mortality / material return:
  Adam → return to ground / dust
```

These are heterogeneous. They should not be flattened into one generic `punishment` field if the goal is machine legibility.

A better structural representation preserves the specific relation that changes.

## Material provenance remains distinct

The text says Adam was taken from the ground and will return to dust.

So it contains a material-provenance relation:

```text
ground / dust → material source of Adam
Adam → future return to ground / dust
```

That provenance relation is not identical to the reason relation that explains the changed labor condition.

## OPEN

- physical mechanisms implementing the stated changes;
- temporal granularity of all future statements;
- symbolic/theological identification of the serpent's seed or woman's seed;
- whether every future statement should be read descriptively, normatively, juridically, or in some mixed mode.

# 6. Genesis 3:20–21 — Naming and clothing

## Plain rendering

Adam names his wife Eve because she is described as the mother of all living. The LORD God then makes coats of skins for Adam and his wife and clothes them.

## Naming with explicit reason

```text
Adam → names wife → Eve
reason → she is mother of all living
```

The text therefore supplies another naming operation, but unlike many Genesis 1 naming events it also gives an explicit reason clause.

```math
\boxed{
\text{naming action}
+
\text{stated reason}
}
```

The name itself need not be treated as causing or predicting the stated relation.

## Clothing operation

```text
LORD God → makes coats of skins → for Adam + wife
LORD God → clothes Adam + wife
```

This follows the earlier fig-leaf covering but the text does not explicitly state why one clothing action follows the other or what symbolic meaning should be assigned to it.

The safe structural observation is simply that **covering/clothing appears twice through different agents and materials**.

# 7. Genesis 3:22–24 — Represented future possibility, removal, access control

## Plain rendering

The LORD God says that the man has become like “one of us” in knowing good and evil, then introduces a concern expressed through `lest`: the man might take from the tree of life, eat, and live forever. The man is therefore sent out of Eden to till the ground from which he was taken. He is driven out, and Cherubims plus a flaming sword are placed at the east of the garden to keep the way to the tree of life.

## Represented future branch

The `lest` clause represents a possible sequence:

```text
man → put forth hand
man → take from tree of life
man → eat
man → live for ever
```

This is directly useful machine-readable structure even without deciding whether every action was physically executable at every moment.

The important fact is:

```math
\boxed{
\text{a future branch is represented in the discourse}
}
```

## Cross-verse correspondence

Genesis 3:5 contains:

```text
knowing good and evil
```

and Genesis 3:22 contains the same core relation:

```text
man → know good and evil
```

Again, the correspondence can be preserved without forcing a complete interpretation of `as gods` / `as one of us`.

## Removal and role change

```text
LORD God → sends man out of Eden
man → assigned / directed activity → till ground
material provenance → ground from which man was taken
```

This is a major environment-agent transition:

```math
\boxed{
\text{agent inside garden with prior access relations}
\rightarrow
\text{agent outside garden with changed work relation}
}
```

## Access-control structure

The chapter ends with:

```text
man → driven out
Cherubims → placed east of garden
flaming sword → placed / turning every way
purpose → keep the way of the tree of life
```

A low-assumption structural label is:

```math
\boxed{
\text{explicit access restriction / guarding arrangement}
}
```

This is stronger and more useful than merely saying that the chapter ends with a location change. The text explicitly supplies a guarded path and its purpose.

# 8. Chapter-level state transition

A compact chapter model is:

```math
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
S_0 &: \text{garden state with prior rule / prohibition}\\
&\downarrow\\
R_1 &: \text{competing representations of action consequences}\\
&\downarrow\\
E_1 &: \text{woman evaluates tree}\\
&\downarrow\\
A_1 &: \text{woman eats; husband eats}\\
&\downarrow\\
S_1 &: \text{opened eyes / knowledge of nakedness}\\
&\downarrow\\
A_2 &: \text{covering + hiding}\\
&\downarrow\\
Q_1 &: \text{information-source and action inquiry}\\
&\downarrow\\
\Pi_1 &: \text{upstream action / influence accounts}\\
&\downarrow\\
C_1 &: \text{stated changed conditions / future consequences}\\
&\downarrow\\
S_2 &: \text{removal from garden + guarded access to tree of life}
\end{aligned}
}
```

This is intentionally lossy, but it captures the chapter's dominant transitions.

# 9. What Genesis 3 adds to the structural vocabulary

Without claiming that these categories are universal, Genesis 3 makes the following structures useful:

```text
competing represented claims
reported source attribution
permission / prohibition
prospective consequence
agent evaluation
performed action
resulting state
knowledge / self-representation
concealment
information-source query
action query
upstream account
influence/deception attribution
explicit reason → consequence edge
changed agent-environment relation
represented future branch
access control / guarding
```

The important methodological point is that these categories are **earned by the passage**. They do not need to have been specified before reading it.

# 10. Repeated patterns inside the chapter

## Pattern A — speech changes the represented decision environment

Before the eating action, the chapter spends several verses on incompatible descriptions of what the action means and what will follow from it.

```math
\boxed{
\text{represented rule / consequence}
\rightarrow
\text{competing representation}
\rightarrow
\text{evaluation}
\rightarrow
\text{action}
}
```

The text thereby distinguishes the physical tree from the **representations attached to acting on it**.

## Pattern B — action changes what becomes salient afterward

Before eating, the tree's food/visual/wisdom properties dominate the woman's evaluation.

After eating, the text immediately foregrounds:

```text
nakedness
covering
fear
hiding
```

So the chapter's represented state changes not only in external relations but also in what the agents know and respond to.

## Pattern C — forward action is followed by backward explanation

The first half moves forward:

```text
claim → evaluation → action → consequence
```

The inquiry section then moves backward:

```text
current condition → source query → action query → upstream account
```

That forward/backward symmetry is one of the cleanest structural patterns in the chapter.

## Pattern D — consequences alter future interaction structure

The final consequences do not merely assign isolated properties. They modify relations:

```text
serpent ↔ woman / seed
woman ↔ childbirth / husband
Adam ↔ ground / labor / food
human ↔ garden
human ↔ tree of life access
```

Genesis 3 therefore ends in a materially different relational graph from the one it begins with.

# 11. Plain-English chapter compression

A non-theological, legibility-first paraphrase of the chapter's structure is:

> A rule about a prohibited action already exists. A second speaker challenges the stated consequence of that rule and supplies a competing future description. The woman evaluates the relevant object, acts, and the man acts with her. New states of awareness are reported, followed by covering and hiding. God questions the humans about their information and actions; they provide upstream accounts. God then states consequences that change bodily, social, environmental, and future conditions. The humans are ultimately removed from the garden, and access to the tree of life is guarded.

That is not intended to replace the KJV. It is the chapter rendered into a form where the underlying operations are easier to see.

---

# 12. Interpretation boundary

The structural parse above does **not** by itself settle questions such as:

```text
What exactly is the serpent?
What is the theological nature of the event?
What does “knowledge of good and evil” ultimately mean?
What is the significance of nakedness?
What do the garments symbolize?
How should the serpent/woman seed language be interpreted?
What is the theological meaning of death in the chapter?
What is the relation between Genesis 3 and later biblical texts?
```

Those are interpretation-layer questions.

The point of the structural layer is to make them easier to ask without first fighting the archaic surface syntax.

---

# 13. Carry-forward questions for Genesis 4

Genesis 4 can now be approached without forcing Genesis 3's structure onto it.

Useful questions to carry forward are:

```text
What new actors and roles appear?
What new evaluation / affect states appear?
How are offerings represented?
What explicit warnings or conditional structures appear?
What actions are performed?
How is responsibility queried or attributed?
What consequences alter later possibilities?
Which structural patterns recur from Genesis 3, and which disappear?
```

The guiding rule remains:

```math
\boxed{\textbf{Make it legible first. Understand what is there second. Interpret third.}}
```
