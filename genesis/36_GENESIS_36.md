# Genesis 36 — Legibility-First Structural Decoding

**Source:** pinned King James Version (KJV) corpus  
**Purpose:** make the chapter legible before interpretation  
**Status:** working structural reading; categories may evolve when later text requires them

The governing boundary remains:

```math
\boxed{\text{TEXT} \neq \text{STRUCTURAL PARSE} \neq \text{INTERPRETATION}}
```

and the working question for this chapter is:

```math
\boxed{\textbf{How does Genesis 36 transform one lineage into a population-and-authority structure?}}
```

Genesis 36 should not be forced into the event-chain grammar that dominated Genesis 27–35. Its native structure is closer to a set of linked records with repeated but differently typed fields. It begins with Esau/Edom, wives, sons, and a migration from Jacob caused by an explicit shared-capacity problem. It then restates Esau's descendants in Mount Seir, reclassifies descendants as dukes, introduces Seir the Horite and a second local population graph, lists Horite dukes, shifts again into a political succession list of Edomite kings, and closes with another duke classification indexed by families, places, names, habitations, and the land of possession.

The chapter therefore requires typed edges rather than one generic genealogy relation.

A useful representation is:

```math
\boxed{
G=(V,
E_{spouse},
E_{parent},
E_{sibling},
E_{concubine},
E_{household},
E_{migration},
E_{inhabit},
E_{duke},
E_{family},
E_{place},
E_{king},
E_{succession},
E_{territory})
}
```

The central discipline is:

```math
\boxed{
\text{genealogical relation}
\neq
\text{authority relation}
\neq
\text{territorial relation}
\neq
\text{political succession relation}
}
```

A second discipline is:

```math
\boxed{
\text{same surface name}
\neq
\text{proved same entity}
}
```

A third is:

```math
\boxed{
\text{field absent from a record}
\neq
\text{field false}
}
```

And a fourth is:

```math
\boxed{
\text{repeated record shape}
\neq
\text{repeated relation type}
}
```

Genesis 5 used repetition primarily for biological descent and lifespan. Genesis 36 reuses repetition for genealogy, authority classification, territorial organization, and political succession.

# 1. Genesis 36:1–5 — Esau/Edom, wives, sons, and local Canaan birth records

## Plain rendering

The chapter opens: "these are the generations of Esau, who is Edom." It lists three wives: Adah daughter of Elon the Hittite; Aholibamah daughter of Anah, daughter of Zibeon the Hivite; and Bashemath, daughter of Ishmael and sister of Nebajoth. It then assigns sons to each wife: Adah bears Eliphaz; Bashemath bears Reuel; Aholibamah bears Jeush, Jaalam, and Korah. These sons are explicitly said to have been born to Esau in the land of Canaan.

## Esau and Edom

The text says:

```text
Esau → "who is Edom"
```

and later:

```text
Esau → father of Edomites
land → Edom
chiefs/dukes → Edom
```

This requires type-sensitive representation.

The phrase:

```math
\boxed{\text{Esau is Edom}}
```

is explicit at the person/name level.

But later uses of `Edom` include a people-group and land context. Therefore:

```math
\boxed{
\text{person/name relation}
\neq
\text{people-group relation}
\neq
\text{territorial label}
}
```

Do not flatten all uses of `Edom` into one untyped node.

## Local wife records

Genesis 36 gives:

```text
Adah
→ daughter of Elon the Hittite
→ wife of Esau

Aholibamah
→ daughter of Anah
→ granddaughter of Zibeon the Hivite
→ wife of Esau

Bashemath
→ daughter of Ishmael
→ sister of Nebajoth
→ wife of Esau
```

The chapter then gives child relations:

```text
Adah → Eliphaz
Bashemath → Reuel
Aholibamah → Jeush + Jaalam + Korah
```

These are chapter-local records.

## Cross-chapter wife-record caution

Earlier Genesis records use different wife-name/parent formulations.

Genesis 26:34 records:

```text
Judith → daughter of Beeri the Hittite
Bashemath → daughter of Elon the Hittite
```

Genesis 28:9 records:

```text
Mahalath → daughter of Ishmael
→ sister of Nebajoth
```

Genesis 36 records:

```text
Adah → daughter of Elon the Hittite
Bashemath → daughter of Ishmael
→ sister of Nebajoth
Aholibamah → daughter of Anah → daughter of Zibeon
```

The overlapping parent/sibling relations make cross-chapter identity hypotheses possible, but the structural layer must preserve the source records first.

Therefore:

```math
\boxed{
\text{same parent/sibling pattern}
\neq
\text{proved textual identity of differently named wife records}
}
```

and:

```math
\boxed{
\text{same wife-name surface form across chapters}
\neq
\text{automatic same local record}
}
```

Cross-chapter harmonization remains OPEN unless a later textual bridge earns it.

## Birth-location relation

Verse 5 explicitly says the listed sons:

```text
→ were born unto Esau
→ in land of Canaan
```

Keep:

```math
\boxed{
\text{parent relation}
\neq
\text{birth-location relation}
}
```

# 2. Genesis 36:6–8 — Household/resource expansion becomes spatial separation

## Plain rendering

Esau takes his wives, sons, daughters, all persons of his house, cattle, beasts, and substance acquired in Canaan and moves away from Jacob. The text explicitly gives the reason: their riches were too great for them to dwell together, and the land of their sojourning could not bear them because of their cattle. Esau then dwells in Mount Seir; the text repeats, "Esau is Edom."

## Migrating state bundle

Esau moves with:

```text
wives
sons
daughters
persons of house
cattle
beasts
substance acquired in Canaan
```

Thus migration applies to a composite household/resource system, not merely to Esau as an isolated person.

```math
\boxed{
\text{person movement}
+
\text{household movement}
+
\text{resource movement}
}
```

## Explicit shared-capacity explanation

The chapter gives:

```text
Esau + Jacob riches → too great to dwell together
land of sojourning → could not bear them
explicit reason → cattle
```

So:

```math
\boxed{
\text{resource abundance}
\rightarrow
\text{shared-capacity insufficiency}
\rightarrow
\text{spatial separation}
}
```

This is genuinely textually stated.

It resembles Genesis 13's Abram/Lot capacity conflict, but Genesis 36's local actors and population scale remain distinct.

```math
\boxed{
G13:\text{Abram/Lot separation}
\neq
G36:\text{Esau/Jacob population-resource separation}
}
```

## From departure relation to territorial dwelling

The chapter says:

```text
Esau → went into country from face of Jacob
Esau → dwelt in Mount Seir
```

Thus:

```math
\boxed{
\text{departure from co-location}
\neq
\text{later dwelling relation}
}
```

The exact route between those states is not narrated.

# 3. Genesis 36:9–14 — Esau's descendants are restated in Mount Seir with a deeper genealogical layer

## Plain rendering

The chapter restarts: "these are the generations of Esau the father of the Edomites in mount Seir." It repeats Eliphaz and Reuel as Esau's sons, then expands their descendants. Eliphaz has Teman, Omar, Zepho, Gatam, and Kenaz. Timna is Eliphaz's concubine and bears Amalek. Reuel has Nahath, Zerah, Shammah, and Mizzah. Aholibamah's sons Jeush, Jaalam, and Korah are repeated.

## Repetition is not duplicate noise

Verses 1–5 gave first-generation sons.

Verses 9–14 restate those sons while expanding the next generation and introducing a concubine relation.

Therefore:

```math
\boxed{
\text{earlier local record}
\neq
\text{later expanded restatement}
}
```

The later record adds fields; it does not erase the earlier one.

## Concubine relation

The text gives:

```text
Timna → concubine of Eliphaz
Timna → bears Amalek to Eliphaz
```

So:

```math
\boxed{
E_{concubine}
\neq
E_{spouse/wife}
\neq
E_{parent}
}
```

Even when these relations connect the same local family graph, they should remain typed.

## Descendant branches

A compact graph is:

```text
Esau / Edom
├── Adah
│   └── Eliphaz
│       ├── Teman
│       ├── Omar
│       ├── Zepho
│       ├── Gatam
│       ├── Kenaz
│       └── Amalek ← Timna/Eliphaz
├── Bashemath
│   └── Reuel
│       ├── Nahath
│       ├── Zerah
│       ├── Shammah
│       └── Mizzah
└── Aholibamah
    ├── Jeush
    ├── Jaalam
    └── Korah
```

This graph is genealogical only. Authority status has not yet been added to every node.

# 4. Genesis 36:15–19 — Genealogical descendants are reclassified through duke/authority records

## Plain rendering

The chapter now says, "These were dukes of the sons of Esau," and lists duke Teman, Omar, Zepho, Kenaz, Korah, Gatam, and Amalek from the Eliphaz branch; Nahath, Zerah, Shammah, and Mizzah from Reuel; and Jeush, Jaalam, and Korah from Aholibamah. It closes: "These are the sons of Esau, who is Edom, and these are their dukes."

## Organizational-level shift

The preceding records answer mainly:

```text
Who descends from whom?
```

This section adds:

```text
Who is classified as duke?
```

Therefore:

```math
\boxed{
\text{genealogical membership}
\neq
\text{duke/authority status}
}
```

Do not represent:

```text
son/descendant → therefore duke
```

as a causal rule. The text supplies both relations for certain named records; it does not state a general selection mechanism for ducal authority.

## Same-name trap: Korah

Korah appears in more than one local context:

```text
Aholibamah → bears Korah to Esau (v14)
```

while the Eliphaz-duke list includes:

```text
duke Korah → among dukes said to come of Eliphaz (vv15–16)
```

The text also lists:

```text
duke Korah → among dukes from Aholibamah (v18)
```

A parser must not silently collapse all surface `Korah` occurrences into one entity or silently create multiple entities without marking the ambiguity.

Thus:

```math
\boxed{
\text{Korah}_{surface}
\neq
\text{proved unique entity identifier}
}
```

and:

```math
\boxed{
\text{exact identity mapping among Korah records}=\mathrm{OPEN}
}
```

This is a strong example of:

```math
\boxed{
\textbf{name token is not a primary key.}
}
```

## Authority records retain branch provenance

The chapter groups dukes by:

```text
Eliphaz branch
Reuel branch
Aholibamah branch
```

So a useful typed record is:

```text
person/name
+ branch provenance
+ duke status
+ land-of-Edom context
```

rather than a flat list of titles.

# 5. Genesis 36:20–30 — A second population graph: Seir the Horite, inhabitants, descendants, unusual local record, and Horite dukes

## Plain rendering

The chapter shifts away from Esau's immediate genealogy and introduces "the sons of Seir the Horite, who inhabited the land": Lotan, Shobal, Zibeon, Anah, Dishon, Ezer, and Dishan. These are also called dukes of the Horites, children of Seir in the land of Edom. Their descendants are listed. Lotan's sister Timna is named. A special record says Anah found "the mules" in the wilderness while feeding his father Zibeon's asses. Aholibamah is listed as daughter of Anah. The section ends with another duke classification of Horite names in the land of Seir.

## Population graph changes root

The graph root shifts from:

```text
Esau / Edom
```

to:

```text
Seir the Horite
```

This is not a mere continuation of the Esau father→child tree.

```math
\boxed{
G_{Esau-line}
\neq
G_{Seir/Horite-line}
}
```

The graphs coexist in the same territorial frame.

## Inhabit relation

The text explicitly says the sons of Seir:

```text
→ inhabited the land
```

That gives a new relation type:

```math
\boxed{
E_{inhabit}
\neq
E_{parent}
\neq
E_{duke}
}
```

A person/group can be related genealogically, politically, and territorially in different ways.

## Seir/Horite descendant graph

A compact local graph is:

```text
Seir the Horite
├── Lotan
│   ├── Hori
│   └── Hemam
│   sister: Timna
├── Shobal
│   ├── Alvan
│   ├── Manahath
│   ├── Ebal
│   ├── Shepho
│   └── Onam
├── Zibeon
│   ├── Ajah
│   └── Anah
│       ├── Dishon
│       └── Aholibamah
├── Dishon
│   ├── Hemdan
│   ├── Eshban
│   ├── Ithran
│   └── Cheran
├── Ezer
│   ├── Bilhan
│   ├── Zaavan
│   └── Akan
└── Dishan
    ├── Uz
    └── Aran
```

This should remain a typed local record, not forcibly merged with every same-name node elsewhere in Genesis 36.

## Same-name traps expand

The chapter contains potentially confusable names such as:

```text
Anah
Dishon
Timna
Aholibamah
```

appearing in different relation positions.

Thus:

```math
\boxed{
\text{same lexical name}
+\text{different parent/role context}
\rightarrow
\text{identity requires explicit disambiguation}
}
```

## The Anah wilderness record

Verse 24 says:

```text
Anah
→ fed asses of Zibeon his father
→ found "the mules" in wilderness
```

Preserve the KJV surface category `mules`.

Do not import a biological, lexical, or historical theory of what the underlying term must mean in this structural pass.

```math
\boxed{
\text{event record}
\neq
\text{later interpretive identification of object/category}
}
```

## Horite dukes

Verses 29–30 classify:

```text
Lotan
Shobal
Zibeon
Anah
Dishon
Ezer
Dishan
```

as dukes from the Horites in the land of Seir.

Again:

```math
\boxed{
\text{Horite descent}
\neq
\text{duke classification}

}
```

# 6. Genesis 36:31 — Political state is positioned relative to a different population's future kingship

The chapter now says:

```text
these kings → reigned in land of Edom
before → any king reigned over children of Israel
```

This is not merely a king list introduction. It creates a cross-population temporal comparison.

```math
\boxed{
\text{Edomite kingship state}
\prec
\text{Israelite kingship state}
}
```

at the level of the chapter's explicit temporal wording.

But:

```math
\boxed{
\text{relative temporal ordering}
\neq
\text{complete absolute chronology}
}
```

No dates are supplied here.

It also shows that the chapter has moved organizational level again:

```text
family genealogy
→ ducal authority
→ local inhabitants
→ kingship
```

The representation must move with it.

# 7. Genesis 36:32–39 — Political succession template: reign, place/origin, death, successor

## Plain rendering

Bela son of Beor reigns in Edom, with Dinhabah as his city. Bela dies; Jobab son of Zerah of Bozrah reigns in his stead. Jobab dies; Husham of the land of Temani succeeds. Husham dies; Hadad son of Bedad, who smote Midian in the field of Moab, succeeds, with Avith as his city. Hadad dies; Samlah of Masrekah succeeds. Samlah dies; Saul of Rehoboth by the river succeeds. Saul dies; Baalhanan son of Achbor succeeds. Baalhanan dies; Hadar succeeds, with Pau as his city; Hadar's wife Mehetabel and her maternal line are supplied. The list ends without narrating Hadar's death.

## Political succession is not genealogy

The repeated template is approximately:

```text
king X
→ reigns
→ optional father/origin/city/event fields
→ X dies
→ king Y reigns in X's stead
```

The crucial relation is:

```math
\boxed{E_{succession}}
```

not:

```math
E_{parent}.
```

For example:

```text
Bela dies
→ Jobab reigns in his stead
```

but the chapter does not say:

```text
Jobab → son of Bela
```

Therefore:

```math
\boxed{
\text{successor}
\neq
\text{descendant}
}
```

unless a parent relation is separately supplied.

This is one of Genesis 36's strongest anti-flattening rules.

## King-list records are sparse and nonuniform

Different kings receive different fields:

```text
Bela → father + city
Jobab → father + place association
Husham → territorial origin
Hadad → father + prior military action + city
Samlah → origin
Saul → origin/location
Baalhanan → father
Hadar → city + wife + wife's maternal lineage
```

Thus:

```math
\boxed{
\text{field absent}
\neq
\text{field false}
}
```

A missing city for a king is not evidence that he had no city. A missing father record is not evidence of no father relation. The record is simply sparse.

## `died → reigned in his stead` template

For most transitions:

```math
\boxed{
K_i\ \text{dies}
\rightarrow
K_{i+1}\ \text{reigns in his stead}
}
```

This resembles Genesis 5's repeated `lived → begat → died` rhythm at the level of repetition, but the underlying relation is political succession rather than biological descent.

Therefore:

```math
\boxed{
\text{template similarity}
\neq
\text{relation-type identity}
}
```

## Final king record does not include death

The text says:

```text
Baalhanan dies
→ Hadar reigns in his stead
```

and then supplies Hadar's city and wife relations.

It does not say:

```text
Hadar dies
```

inside this list.

So:

```math
\boxed{
\text{prior repeated death field}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{infer missing final death event}
}
```

Again:

```math
\boxed{
\text{field absent}\neq\text{false, but also}\neq\text{licensed inference}
}
```

# 8. Genesis 36:40–43 — Final duke compression by families, places, names, habitations, and possession

## Plain rendering

The chapter closes with another list of dukes "that came of Esau," explicitly classified "according to their families, after their places, by their names." It names Timnah, Alvah, Jetheth, Aholibamah, Elah, Pinon, Kenaz, Teman, Mibzar, Magdiel, and Iram. These are called dukes of Edom "according to their habitations in the land of their possession." The chapter closes: "he is Esau the father of the Edomites."

## Final records are multidimensional

The text itself names several indexing dimensions:

```text
families
places
names
habitations
land of possession
```

Therefore the final duke list is not safely representable as merely:

```text
Esau → descendants
```

A better record shape is:

```text
duke-name
+ family relation
+ place relation
+ habitation relation
+ Edom possession/territory context
```

Even where the text does not fill every field separately for every name.

## Do not force final dukes to be identical with earlier same-name genealogical records

The final list contains names also seen earlier, including:

```text
Aholibamah
Kenaz
Teman
```

But the final section classifies dukes by families/places/names/habitations.

Therefore:

```math
\boxed{
\text{same surface name in earlier genealogy}
\neq
\text{proved same entity as final territorial-duke record}
}
```

Exact identity mapping remains OPEN unless context is sufficient to disambiguate.

## Land possession is not identical to prior birth or dwelling location

The chapter has now used several place relations:

```text
sons born in Canaan
Esau moves away from Jacob
Esau dwells in Mount Seir
Horites inhabit land
kings reign in land of Edom
final dukes → habitations in land of possession
```

These are different edges:

```math
\boxed{
E_{birthplace}
\neq
E_{movement}
\neq
E_{dwelling}
\neq
E_{inhabit}
\neq
E_{reign-territory}
\neq
E_{possession/habitation}
}
```

Do not collapse them into generic `LOCATED_IN` without retaining relation type.

## Graph A — Esau household / first-generation descent

```text
Esau = Edom
├── spouse: Adah
│   └── Eliphaz
├── spouse: Bashemath
│   └── Reuel
└── spouse: Aholibamah
    ├── Jeush
    ├── Jaalam
    └── Korah
```

## Graph B — expanded Esau branches

```text
Eliphaz
├── Teman
├── Omar
├── Zepho
├── Gatam
├── Kenaz
└── Amalek ← via Timna, Eliphaz's concubine

Reuel
├── Nahath
├── Zerah
├── Shammah
└── Mizzah
```

## Graph C — authority overlay

```text
selected names / branch records
→ classified as dukes
→ in land of Edom
```

Do not derive this overlay automatically from descent.

## Graph D — Seir/Horite population graph

```text
Seir the Horite
→ descendants
→ inhabit land

```

```text
Bela
→ Jobab
→ Husham
→ Hadad
→ Samlah
→ Saul
→ Baalhanan
→ Hadar
```

where each arrow means:

```text
previous king dies
→ next king reigns in his stead
```

not parent→child.

## Graph F — final family/place/duke classification

```text
Esau/Edom
→ dukes
→ families
→ places
→ habitations
→ land of possession
```

The chapter's information is lost if all six graphs are compressed into one descendant tree.

# 10. Entity-resolution hazards

Genesis 36 is one of the strongest entity-resolution stress tests so far.

## Hazard 1 — names are not primary keys

Repeated names include records such as:

```text
Korah
Anah
Dishon
Timna
Aholibamah
Kenaz
Teman
```

The parser must use local relation context.

```math
\boxed{
EntityID \neq NameString
}
```

A safer candidate key is contextual:

```text
(name, parent/branch, relation type, local section, place/role)
```

while preserving OPEN identity when evidence remains insufficient.

## Hazard 2 — same person can have multiple typed labels

Esau is explicitly called Edom, while `Edom` also appears in people/land/authority contexts.

Thus labels require type and scope.

## Hazard 3 — wife-record harmonization

Genesis 26, 28, and 36 contain overlapping but non-identical wife-name/parent records.

Do not overwrite one local record with another.

## Hazard 4 — genealogy versus succession

A king who succeeds another is not thereby his son.

## Hazard 5 — title versus ancestry

A duke status does not imply a new genealogical edge.

## Hazard 6 — place relation overload

Birthplace, dwelling, inhabiting, reigning, and possession are different relations.

# 11. Repetition templates across Genesis

Genesis 36 reveals that repeated textual templates can preserve different semantic edge types.

## Genesis 5 — biological/lifespan template

```text
person
→ age
→ descendant
→ years after
→ total lifespan
→ death
```

## Genesis 10 — population topology

```text
ancestor
→ descendants
→ family/language/land/nation
```

## Genesis 36 — multiple templates

### Genealogical

```text
person
→ spouse/concubine
→ child
→ descendant branch
```

### Authority classification

```text
branch/name
→ duke status
→ land/family context
```

### Political succession

```text
king
→ optional origin/city/family/event fields
→ death
→ successor reigns in his stead
```

### Territorial compression

```text
duke
→ family
→ place
→ habitation
→ land of possession
```

Therefore:

```math
\boxed{
\textbf{template recurrence is structural; relation semantics remain local.}
}
```

This is stronger than saying merely that Genesis repeats formulas.

# 12. Cross-chapter continuities

## Genesis 13 / Genesis 36 — abundance and spatial separation

Genesis 13:

```text
Abram + Lot possessions
→ land unable to bear shared dwelling
→ separation
```

Genesis 36:

```text
Esau + Jacob riches
→ land unable to bear shared dwelling because cattle
→ separation
```

Strong structural recurrence:

```math
\boxed{
\text{resource abundance}
\rightarrow
\text{shared-capacity problem}
\rightarrow
\text{spatial separation}
}
```

But:

```math
\boxed{
\text{recurring structure}
\neq
\text{identical local event or mechanism}
}
```

## Genesis 10 / Genesis 36 — population topology with different emphasized dimensions

Genesis 10 emphasizes:

```text
families
languages
lands
nations
```

Genesis 36 emphasizes:

```text
families
chiefs/dukes
habitations
territory
kings
succession
```

Thus:

```math
\boxed{
\text{population-topology grammar recurs}
+
\text{active dimensions change}
}
```

## Genesis 35 / Genesis 36 — event chain to database graph

Genesis 35 is dominated by state transitions:

```text
remove → move → encounter → name → birth/death → burial
```

Genesis 36 changes representation regime:

```text
records → groups → authority → territory → succession
```

The parser should change with the text rather than forcing one universal narrative schema.

# 13. OPEN edges / non-inferences

## OPEN 1 — Cross-chapter wife identity harmonization

Do not automatically identify Adah with a differently named earlier wife, Bashemath with Mahalath, or any other wife records solely from overlapping parent relations.

## OPEN 2 — Repeated-name entity identity

Do not collapse repeated `Korah`, `Anah`, `Dishon`, `Aholibamah`, `Timna`, `Kenaz`, or `Teman` records without local evidence.

## OPEN 3 — Duke-selection mechanism

The chapter says who the dukes are. It does not supply a general rule explaining why those persons/records hold authority.

## OPEN 4 — Relation between Esau/Edom and Horite population structure beyond stated links

The chapter places both population graphs in the same broad land/Seir/Edom frame and includes relation overlaps such as Aholibamah ancestry. Do not invent a complete ethnic/political merger model.

## OPEN 5 — `mules` mechanism/lexical theory

Preserve the KJV surface record of Anah finding `the mules` in the wilderness. Interpretive identification is outside the local structural pass.

## OPEN 6 — Absolute chronology of kings

Verse 31 supplies relative ordering before Israelite kingship, not absolute dates.

## OPEN 7 — Hereditary kingship

Succession `in his stead` does not imply father→son transmission.

## OPEN 8 — Identity of final duke list with earlier same-name persons

Final records are classified by family/place/name/habitation. Same names do not by themselves prove entity identity.

## OPEN 9 — Missing fields

No missing father, city, spouse, child, territory, or death field should be inferred as false or filled from pattern alone.

# 14. Chapter compression

Genesis 36 should not be compressed to:

```text
Esau's descendants
```

The stronger representation is:

```math
\boxed{
\text{person/household}
\rightarrow
\text{descendant branches}
\rightarrow
\text{resource-driven geographic separation}
\rightarrow
\text{population groups}
\rightarrow
\text{authority overlays}
\rightarrow
\text{territorial organization}
\rightarrow
\text{political succession}
\rightarrow
\text{family/place/habitation summary}
}
```

But even that arrow chain is only a chapter-level compression. The native representation remains a typed graph/database.

The strongest methodological lesson is:

```math
\boxed{
\textbf{When the text changes organizational level, change the representation level with it.}
}
```

A second is:

```math
\boxed{
\textbf{Repeated record shape does not imply repeated relation type.}
}
```

A third is:

```math
\boxed{
\textbf{Name strings are labels, not guaranteed entity identifiers.}
}
```

And a fourth is:

```math
\boxed{
\textbf{Sparse records must remain sparse: absence of a field is not a license to fill it from the surrounding template.}
}
```

Genesis 36 therefore expands the corpus ontology from family relations into a genuinely multi-layer representation containing genealogy, migration, inhabitation, authority, territory, and succession without collapsing those dimensions into one relation.

**Decode the structure, not just the names.**
